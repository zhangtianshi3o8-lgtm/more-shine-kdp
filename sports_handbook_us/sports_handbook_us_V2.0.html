Building the Foundation
Sports Fundamentals
Before your child picks up a ball or steps on a field, they need the building blocks of athletic development. This section covers the science of how young bodies grow, proper warm-up routines, flexibility training, nutrition that fuels performance, and hydration essentials. Understanding these fundamentals helps parents create a safe, supportive environment where athletic talent can flourish naturally.

Part One &middot; Building the Foundation Understanding Athletic Development
Understanding Athletic Development
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Ages 6-9: Focus on fundamental movement skills (running, jumping, throwing) through fun games and play
Ages 10-12: Window for speed and agility development — nervous system is highly adaptable
Ages 13-15: Strength development becomes effective as hormones shift; prioritize form over weight
Ages 16+: Sport-specific training and competitive skills can be fully developed
Early specialization in one sport before age 12 increases burnout and injury risk
Practical Tips for Parents
Let your child try multiple sports — cross-training builds better overall athleticism
Watch for natural coordination patterns: some kids excel at power movements, others at endurance
Keep practice sessions short and positive — 30 minutes of focused activity beats 2 hours of drills
Celebrate effort and improvement, not just wins and scores

Part One &middot; Building the Foundation Dynamic Warm-Up Techniques
Dynamic Warm-Up Techniques
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Dynamic stretching before activity prepares muscles and joints for movement
A proper warm-up takes 8-12 minutes and raises heart rate gradually
Walking lunges, high knees, butt kicks, and arm circles are effective warm-up movements
Warm-ups should mimic the movements of the upcoming sport
Cold muscles are more prone to strains and tears
Practical Tips for Parents
Make warm-ups a consistent routine before every practice and game
Use a timer or count repetitions to keep warm-ups structured
Lead by example — warm up alongside your child
Keep it fun: turn warm-up exercises into a game or challenge

Part One &middot; Building the Foundation Stretching and Flexibility
Stretching and Flexibility
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Static stretching is best done after activity when muscles are warm
Hold each stretch for 20-30 seconds without bouncing
Major muscle groups to stretch: hamstrings, quadriceps, calves, shoulders, and back
Improved flexibility reduces injury risk and enhances range of motion
Children are naturally flexible but lose flexibility during growth spurts
Practical Tips for Parents
Create a post-activity cool-down routine that includes 5-10 minutes of stretching
Never force a stretch beyond mild discomfort — pain means stop
Stretch both sides of the body equally
Encourage breathing deeply during each stretch to help muscles relax

Part One &middot; Building the Foundation Sports Nutrition for Young Athletes
Sports Nutrition for Young Athletes
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Carbohydrates are the primary fuel for active muscles — whole grains, fruits, and vegetables
Protein supports muscle repair and growth — aim for lean sources with each meal
Healthy fats from nuts, avocados, and olive oil provide sustained energy
Eat a balanced meal 2-3 hours before activity; light snack 30-60 minutes before
Post-exercise recovery snack within 30 minutes helps replenish energy stores
Practical Tips for Parents
Plan pre-game meals around easily digestible foods like pasta, rice, or sandwiches
Keep healthy snacks in your sports bag: fruit, granola bars, or trail mix
Avoid sugary drinks and processed foods before activity — they cause energy crashes
Teach your child to recognize hunger cues and eat regular meals

Part One &middot; Building the Foundation Hydration and Recovery
Hydration and Recovery
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Young athletes should drink water before, during, and after activity
Drink 8-16 ounces of water 30 minutes before exercise
During activity, drink 4-8 ounces every 15-20 minutes
Electrolyte drinks are only needed for activities lasting over 60 minutes in heat
Signs of dehydration include headache, fatigue, dizziness, and dark urine
Practical Tips for Parents
Send your child to practice with a full water bottle every time
Encourage drinking even when not thirsty — thirst is a late indicator
Monitor urine color as a simple hydration check — pale yellow is ideal
Ensure 8-10 hours of sleep per night for proper recovery and growth

Part Two &middot; Core Athletic Skills Speed and Sprint Development
Speed and Sprint Development
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Sprint speed comes from two factors: stride length and stride frequency
Proper arm action drives leg speed — arms should swing forward and back, not across the body
Forward lean from the ankles (not the waist) accelerates the first 10 yards
Relaxation is key — tight muscles are slow muscles
Speed can be trained at any age through short, high-intensity bursts
Practical Tips for Parents
Set up 10-20 yard sprint races in the backyard or park
Use visual targets like cones or chalk marks to give your child something to run toward
Keep speed work short: 5-10 second sprints with full recovery between reps
Film your child running in slow motion to help them see their form

Part Two &middot; Core Athletic Skills Strength and Power Training
Strength and Power Training
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Bodyweight exercises are safe and effective for children of all ages
Push-ups, squats, lunges, and planks build functional strength
Plyometric exercises (jumping, bounding) develop explosive power
Core strength supports every athletic movement and protects the spine
Proper form always comes before adding resistance or repetitions
Practical Tips for Parents
Create a simple circuit: 10 push-ups, 10 squats, 20-second plank, repeat 3 times
Use household items as light weights: water bottles, canned goods
Make strength training a game: who can hold a plank the longest?
Focus on quality over quantity — 5 perfect push-ups beat 15 sloppy ones

Part Two &middot; Core Athletic Skills Agility and Quickness
Agility and Quickness
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Agility is the ability to change direction quickly while maintaining balance
Cone drills, ladder drills, and shuttle runs develop footwork and reaction time
Lateral movement is often neglected but critical in most sports
Quick first steps come from staying on the balls of the feet
Agility training improves both offensive and defensive performance
Practical Tips for Parents
Set up a simple cone course in the yard — zig-zags, circles, and stop-and-go patterns
Call out directions (left, right, forward, back) and have your child react instantly
Use sidewalk chalk to draw a footwork ladder on the driveway
Time the drills and track improvement over weeks to show progress

Part Two &middot; Core Athletic Skills Balance and Coordination
Balance and Coordination
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Balance is the foundation of all skilled movement
Single-leg exercises improve stability and proprioception (body awareness)
Hand-eye coordination develops through catching, throwing, and striking activities
Balance training helps prevent ankle and knee injuries
Coordination skills peak in adaptability during ages 8-12
Practical Tips for Parents
Challenge balance: stand on one foot with eyes closed for 30 seconds
Play catch with different sized balls to develop hand-eye coordination
Use a balance beam or draw a line on the ground for walking exercises
Jump rope is one of the best coordination tools — start with basic bouncing

Part Two &middot; Core Athletic Skills Endurance and Stamina
Endurance and Stamina
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Aerobic endurance allows sustained activity without fatigue
Interval training (alternating hard and easy efforts) builds both speed and stamina
A strong cardiovascular base supports recovery between plays
Endurance is sport-specific: a soccer player needs different stamina than a baseball player
Gradual progression prevents overuse injuries — increase volume by no more than 10% per week
Practical Tips for Parents
Start with 15-20 minutes of continuous activity and build gradually
Mix running with games: tag, capture the flag, or relay races
Use the talk test: your child should be able to speak in short sentences during easy pace
Track weekly activity to ensure steady, safe progression

Part Three &middot; American Sports Guide Basketball
Basketball
Ages 5-6 to start; competitive play begins around age 8
Key Skills to Develop
Ball handling: dribbling with both hands without looking down at the ball
Shooting form: squared shoulders, eyes on the rim, follow through with the wrist
Court vision: awareness of teammates, opponents, and open space
Defensive stance: wide base, active hands, lateral quickness
Passing: chest passes, bounce passes, and overhead passes with accuracy
Talent Indicators
Natural height or long wingspan relative to peers
Quick first step and ability to change direction while dribbling
Good hand-eye coordination — catches passes cleanly
Competitive instinct and willingness to take important shots
Home Practice Drills
Wall dribbling: dribble against a wall with each hand for 1 minute
Form shooting: practice shooting motion lying on the back, rolling ball straight up
Cone dribbling: set up 5 cones and dribble through them switching hands
Defensive slides: shuffle laterally between two points, staying low
EQUIPMENT: Basketball (size appropriate for age), hoop at adjustable height, flat surface for dribbling

Part Three &middot; American Sports Guide Baseball and Softball
Baseball and Softball
Ages 4-6 (T-ball); ages 7-8 (coach pitch); ages 9+ (player pitch)
Key Skills to Develop
Hitting fundamentals: stance, grip, weight transfer, and hip rotation
Throwing mechanics: four-seam grip, elbow above shoulder, follow through across body
Fielding: ready position, two hands for ground balls, tracking fly balls
Base running: explosive first step, proper turn at bases, sliding technique
Game awareness: knowing the situation, pitch count, and defensive positioning
Talent Indicators
Natural hand-eye coordination — makes consistent contact with the ball
Strong, accurate throwing arm for their age
Quick reflexes when fielding grounders or line drives
Left-handed hitting or throwing can be an advantage at higher levels
Home Practice Drills
Tee work: hit off a batting tee into a net or fence, focusing on form
Soft toss: toss balls from the side and have your child hit into a fence
Wall ball: throw against a wall and field the rebound — improves both throwing and fielding
Base running: time 30-yard dashes from home to first base
EQUIPMENT: Glove, bat (correct length and weight), batting helmet, baseballs or softballs, batting tee

Part Three &middot; American Sports Guide Football
Football
Ages 5-7 (flag football); ages 8+ (tackle football with proper coaching)
Key Skills to Develop
Catching: hands form a diamond or basket, secure the ball before running
Route running: crisp cuts, proper depth, and selling the route
Tackling form: head up, wrap arms, drive through the ball carrier (tackle football)
Footwork: three-point stance, first-step explosion, and change of direction
Field awareness: understanding positions, assignments, and reading the play
Talent Indicators
Explosive acceleration and short-area quickness
Physical toughness and willingness to make or absorb contact
Good hands — catches the ball naturally away from the body
Strong spatial awareness and ability to react to moving plays
Home Practice Drills
Passing tree: run and catch each route pattern (slant, out, curl, go)
Cone footwork: explosive 5-yard bursts with sharp direction changes
Ball security: hold a ball while a parent tries to strip it away
Agility ladder: rapid footwork through ladder patterns on the ground
EQUIPMENT: Football (youth size), cones, flag belts (for flag football), cleats, mouthguard
Practice Notes

Part Three &middot; American Sports Guide Soccer
Soccer
Ages 3-5 (introductory programs); ages 6+ (organized leagues)
Key Skills to Develop
Dribbling: close ball control using the inside and outside of the foot
Passing: accurate ground passes using the instep (shoelaces) and inside foot
First touch: receiving the ball cleanly and preparing it for the next action
Shooting: power with the laces, placement with the inside of the foot
Spatial awareness: moving to open space, both with and without the ball
Talent Indicators
Natural comfort with the ball at their feet — dribbles without panic under pressure
Quick, agile footwork and ability to change pace suddenly
Good vision — sees and delivers passes before defenders react
Endurance and willingness to run throughout the game
Home Practice Drills
Cone dribbling: weave through cones using both feet, inside and outside touches
Wall passing: pass against a wall and control the return with one touch
Juggling: keep the ball in the air using feet, thighs, and head — count consecutive touches
Target shooting: place targets in the goal and practice hitting them from various angles
EQUIPMENT: Soccer ball (size 3 for under 8, size 4 for 8-12, size 5 for 13+), cleats, shin guards, cones

Part Three &middot; American Sports Guide Swimming
Swimming
Ages 1-3 (water familiarization); ages 4-5 (basic strokes); ages 6+ (competitive)
Key Skills to Develop
Water safety: comfort in water, floating, and breathing control come first
Freestyle: alternating arm pulls with flutter kick and side breathing
Backstroke: steady flutter kick with alternating arm recovery
Breaststroke: frog kick with simultaneous arm pull and glide
Flip turns and streamline push-offs for competitive swimming
Talent Indicators
Natural buoyancy and comfort in water from an early age
Long limbs and flexible ankles for efficient kicking
Good lung capacity and ability to hold breath comfortably
Competitive drive — enjoys racing and beating personal times
Home Practice Drills
Dry-land kicking: lie on the floor and practice flutter kick and breaststroke kick
Arm circles: practice arm recovery motion standing in front of a mirror
Breathing drills: practice rhythmic exhaling underwater in the bathtub or pool
Core strength: planks and sit-ups build the stability needed for all strokes
EQUIPMENT: Swimsuit, goggles, swim cap (for long hair), access to a pool, kickboard
Practice Notes

Part Three &middot; American Sports Guide Tennis
Tennis
Ages 4-5 (with smaller rackets and red balls); ages 7+ (standard progression)
Key Skills to Develop
Forehand: eastern or semi-western grip, unit turn, contact in front of the body
Backhand: one-handed or two-handed, with proper shoulder rotation
Serve: continental grip, ball toss, kinetic chain from legs to arm
Footwork: split step, recovery steps, and positioning for each shot
Court positioning: baseline for groundstrokes, approach for volleys, net for finishing
Talent Indicators
Excellent hand-eye coordination — tracks and times the ball well
Quick first step and ability to change direction instantly
Patience and focus — can sustain concentration through long points
Competitive temperament — thrives under pressure and adapts to opponents
Home Practice Drills
Wall practice: hit against a wall, focusing on consistent form and rhythm
Shadow swings: practice stroke motion without a ball, checking form in a mirror
Ball toss: practice serving toss — the ball should go straight up and come back down
Agility: cone drills for quick direction changes and split-step practice
EQUIPMENT: Tennis racket (size appropriate for age and height), tennis balls, court access, proper shoes

Part Three &middot; American Sports Guide Track and Field
Track and Field
Ages 6-8 (basic running and jumping); ages 9+ (event-specific training)
Key Skills to Develop
Sprinting: explosive start, drive phase at 70% body lean, maximum velocity mechanics
Distance running: relaxed posture, efficient stride, controlled breathing
Jumping events: approach speed, takeoff, flight technique, and landing
Throwing events: rotational or linear technique, core power, and release angle
Relays: baton exchange technique and team coordination
Talent Indicators
Natural speed — consistently fastest in their peer group over short distances
Explosive jumping ability — can jump high or far relative to body size
High pain tolerance and mental toughness for distance events
Rhythmic coordination for hurdling and jumping events
Home Practice Drills
Sprint intervals: 30-meter sprints with full recovery, focusing on form
Standing long jump: measure distance and track improvement over time
Medicine ball tosses (or weighted ball) for throwing power development
Running form drills: high knees, butt kicks, and A-skips for 20 meters each
EQUIPMENT: Running shoes, stopwatch, measuring tape, cones, access to a track or open field
Practice Notes

Part Three &middot; American Sports Guide Volleyball
Volleyball
Ages 7-9 (modified games with lighter balls); ages 10+ (standard rules)
Key Skills to Develop
Passing (bump): platform with forearms, legs generate power, absorb and direct the ball
Setting: fingertip contact, quick release, push the ball to the target
Serving: underhand for beginners, overhand with toss for advanced players
Hitting (spike): approach footwork, jump timing, and arm swing contact
Blocking: timing the jump, penetrating the net plane with hands
Talent Indicators
Good vertical jump and explosive leg power
Quick reflexes and ability to read the hitter's approach
Height advantage, particularly for hitting and blocking positions
Strong hand-eye coordination for setting and digging
Home Practice Drills
Wall passing: pass against a wall continuously, maintaining a platform
Setting to self: set the ball overhead repeatedly without letting it drop
Serving practice: serve against a wall or over a line, focusing on consistent contact
Approach footwork: practice the three-step hitting approach without a ball
EQUIPMENT: Volleyball (lighter weight for beginners), net (or rope at appropriate height), knee pads
Practice Notes

Part Three &middot; American Sports Guide Golf
Golf
Ages 4-6 (SNAG or plastic clubs); ages 7+ (real clubs with proper instruction)
Key Skills to Develop
Grip: interlocking, overlapping, or baseball grip — choose what feels natural
Stance and alignment: feet shoulder-width, shoulders parallel to target line
Full swing: backswing turn, weight shift, downswing sequence, and follow-through
Short game: chipping, pitching, and putting account for most scoring strokes
Course management: club selection, shot strategy, and mental composure
Talent Indicators
Natural hand-eye coordination and body awareness
Patience and focus — golf requires sustained concentration over 18 holes
Good rhythm and timing in the swing
Emotional control — able to recover from bad shots without frustration
Home Practice Drills
Putting practice: use a cup or target on the floor, focus on distance control
Chipping in the yard: use plastic or foam balls to practice chip shots to a target
Mirror work: practice backswing and finish positions in front of a mirror
Balance drill: hold the finish position for 3 seconds after each swing
EQUIPMENT: Youth golf clubs (cut down or junior set), golf balls, tees, putting mat or access to a course

Part Four &middot; Safety and Health Injury Prevention Strategies
Injury Prevention Strategies
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Proper warm-up and cool-down routines are the single most effective injury prevention tool
Sport-specific conditioning strengthens the muscles and joints most used in each sport
Correct equipment fit is critical — helmets, shoes, and protective gear must be sized properly
Rest days are not optional — growing bodies need 1-2 days off per week from organized sport
Playing multiple sports reduces overuse injuries compared to year-round single-sport participation
Practical Tips for Parents
Inspect equipment regularly for wear, cracks, or improper fit
Learn the common injuries for your child's sport and watch for early warning signs
Encourage communication — your child should feel safe reporting pain without fear of losing playing time
Keep a first aid kit at all games and practices

Part Four &middot; Safety and Health Common Youth Sports Injuries
Common Youth Sports Injuries
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Sprains and strains: stretching or tearing of ligaments and muscles — most common in ankles and knees
Growth plate injuries: damage to developing bone tissue — more common in children than adults
Overuse injuries: repetitive stress causes gradual damage — stress fractures, tendonitis, and Sever's disease
Concussions: brain injuries from head impact — require immediate removal from play and medical evaluation
Heat illness: dehydration and heat exhaustion — prevent with proper hydration and acclimatization
Practical Tips for Parents
Learn the RICE method: Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation for acute injuries
Never let a child play through pain — pain is the body's warning system
After a concussion, follow return-to-play protocols under medical supervision
Seek professional medical evaluation for any injury that causes limping, swelling, or limited motion

Part Four &middot; Safety and Health Recovery and Return to Play
Recovery and Return to Play
Key knowledge for parents
What Parents Need to Know
Active recovery (light walking, swimming, or cycling) promotes blood flow and healing
Sleep is the most powerful recovery tool — 9-11 hours for children under 12
Nutrition post-exercise: protein for muscle repair, carbohydrates for energy replenishment
Gradual return to sport follows a step-by-step progression from light activity to full competition
Mental recovery matters too — burnout and loss of motivation are real concerns in youth sports
Practical Tips for Parents
Schedule mandatory rest days each week — no exceptions
Watch for signs of burnout: declining performance, mood changes, or reluctance to practice
Encourage off-season activities that are not sport-related to maintain balance
Keep a training log to track workload and ensure gradual progression
