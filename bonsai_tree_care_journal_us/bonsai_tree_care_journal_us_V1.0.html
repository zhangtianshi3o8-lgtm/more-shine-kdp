Getting Started More Shine Press
How to Use This Journal
The art of patient cultivation, one record at a time
Why Keep a Bonsai Journal?
Bonsai is a relationship measured in decades, not days. A journal captures the slow unfolding of each tree's story — when you pruned, how it responded, what the weather did, and how your skills grew alongside your collection. Without records, years of learning fade. With them, every tree becomes a teacher.
Guidelines
1. Log on the same day. Record work immediately after performing it. Details like soil moisture level, exact branch angles, and fertilizer ratios fade quickly from memory.
2. Photograph before and after. A photo from the same angle each season reveals growth that is invisible day to day. Note the photo date in your journal entry.
3. Track the seasons. Bonsai care follows nature's calendar. Record when buds break, when fall color peaks, and when your tree goes dormant. Patterns emerge over the years.
4. Note your mistakes honestly. A dead tree teaches more than a healthy one. Record what went wrong — overwatering, wrong soil, late pruning — so you never repeat it.
5. Plan before you cut. Sketch the tree and draw your intended design before pruning. Every cut is permanent. A drawing helps you see the future silhouette.

Spring Best time for structural pruning. Prune before buds open. Repot when buds swell. Wire new growth. Pine: candle pruning. Juniper: pinch new growth. Major styling OK. Repot late spring. Active growth. Prune and wire freely. Repot as new growth starts.

Golden Rules of Pruning
1. Prune to enhance the tree's natural character, not to force an unnatural shape.
2. Never remove more than 1/3 of foliage in a single session.
3. Always cut to a node or branch collar. Flush cuts leave wounds.
4. Use sharp, clean tools. Disinfect between trees.
5. Seal large cuts with cut paste on deciduous trees to prevent dieback.
Bonsai Tree Care Journal 5
Reference Watering Fundamentals
Watering Guide
The most critical skill in bonsai
The Golden Rule
Water when the soil surface begins to feel dry — not on a fixed schedule. Soil, weather, species, pot size, and season all change how fast water is used. Learn to read each tree individually.
How to Water Properly
1. Check before watering. Push your finger 1/2 inch into the soil. If it feels moist, wait. If it feels dry, water thoroughly. The surface can look dry while the root ball is still wet.
2. Water twice. Water the entire surface until water runs from the drainage holes. Wait a minute, then water again. The first pass moistens the surface; the second penetrates the root ball.
3. Use a fine rose. A watering can with a fine rose attachment prevents soil erosion and ensures even distribution. Never blast the soil with a hose nozzle.
4. Morning is best. Water in early morning so excess moisture evaporates during the day. Evening watering can promote fungal problems. Avoid midday watering in summer (hot roots).
Signs of Trouble
SymptomLikely CauseAction
Yellow leaves fallingOverwatering — roots suffocatingCheck drainage. Reduce watering frequency.
Crisp brown edgesUnderwatering or wind burnWater more frequently. Add humidity tray.
Wilted but soil wetRoot rot from chronic overwateringRepot. Trim dead roots. Fresh soil.
Sudden leaf dropShock: temperature, location, or watering changeStabilize conditions. Do not fertilize.
Remember: There is no watering schedule that works every day. A tree may need water twice a day in summer heat and once a week in winter. Always check the soil.

Reference Soil & Repotting
Soil Mixtures & Repotting
The foundation of tree health
Component Function Notes
Akadama Moisture retention Japanese clay. Breaks down over 1-2 years, signaling repot time. Gold standard.
Pumice Drainage / aeration Light volcanic rock. Holds some water and air. Essential component.
Lava Rock (Scoria) Drainage / structure Provides aeration and structural stability. Does not break down.
Kanuma Acidic (azaleas) Japanese acidic soil. Ideal for azaleas and other acid-loving species.
Perlite Budget alternative Accessible drainage additive. Lighter than pumice. Good for beginners.
Bark fines Organic matter Retains moisture and nutrients. Use sparingly in bonsai mixes.
Recommended Mix Ratios
Species Type Akadama Pumice Lava Rock
Conifers (Pine/Juniper)1 part1 part1 part
Deciduous (Maple/Elm)2 parts1 part1 part
Tropical (Ficus/Jade)1 part1 part1 part + bark
Azalea—1 partKanuma 2 parts
When to Repot
Frequency: Young/fast-growing trees every 1-2 years. Mature trees every 3-5 years. Conifers less frequently than deciduous.
Timing: Late winter / early spring, just before buds break. Tropicals: mid-summer when actively growing.
Signs: Water drains slowly. Roots circling the pot. Akadama breaking into mush. Tree lifting from pot.
Process: Remove 1/3 to 1/2 of old soil. Trim 1/3 of roots. Repot with fresh mix. Water thoroughly. Keep in shade 2 weeks.
Bonsai Tree Care Journal 7
Reference Wiring & Styling
Wiring & Styling Guide
Shaping branches with patience and precision
The Purpose of Wiring
Wiring allows you to bend and position branches precisely. Unlike pruning (which removes material), wiring reshapes what already exists. It is the primary technique for creating the artistic form of your bonsai.
Wire Selection
Anodized aluminum: Easier for beginners. Softer, reusable. Good for deciduous and tropical species.
Annealed copper: Hardens when bent. Stronger hold for a thinner gauge. Preferred for conifers.
Rule of thumb: Use wire that is about 1/3 the thickness of the branch you are wiring.
Application Steps
1. Anchor the wire by pushing the end deep into the soil at the trunk base.
2. Wrap at a 45-degree angle, evenly spaced. Keep wire firm but not crushing the bark.
3. Wire in the direction you intend to bend. Clockwise wrap = clockwise bend.
4. Use both hands to bend. Support the branch on both sides of the wire.
5. Check weekly. Remove before the wire cuts into the bark (bark biting).
When to Remove Wire
SpeciesTypical DurationWatch For
Fast-growing (Maple)2-4 months in growing seasonBark swells quickly. Check weekly.
Juniper6-12 monthsSlow to set. Be patient.
Pine6-18 monthsVery slow. Can stay on through dormancy.
Tropical (Ficus)1-3 monthsFast growth. Watch for marks.
Caution: Wire marks are permanent scars on the bark. It is always better to remove wire too early than too late. If in doubt, remove it and rewire later.
Bonsai Tree Care Journal 8
Reference Pests & Diseases
Common Pests & Diseases
Identify and treat problems early
Problem Signs Treatment
Aphids Clusters of tiny green/black insects on new growth. Sticky sap (honeydew) on leaves below. Spray with water. Neem oil. Insecticidal soap. Encourage ladybugs.
Spider Mites Fine webbing between leaves. Yellow stippling on foliage. Worse in dry heat. Spray underside of leaves with water daily. Neem oil. Increase humidity.
Scale Brown bumps on branches and leaves. Sticky residue. Ants farming them. Scrape off manually with toothpick. Systemic insecticide for severe cases.
Mealybugs White cottony masses in leaf joints and branch crotches. Remove with alcohol swab. Neem oil spray. Systemic for persistent cases.
Fungus Gnats Small black flies around soil. Larvae in topsoil damaging fine roots. Allow surface to dry between watering. Yellow sticky traps. BTI drench.
Powdery Mildew White powdery spots on leaves. Worse in high humidity / poor airflow. Improve airflow. Remove affected leaves. Fungicide spray. Reduce humidity.
Root Rot Soft black roots. Foul smell. Tree declining despite adequate water. Repot immediately. Remove all dead roots. Fresh gritty soil. Reduce water.
Leaf Burn Brown crispy leaf edges or tips. Not a pest — environmental. Protect from hot afternoon sun. Check watering. Provide wind protection.
Prevention
• Inspect trees weekly, especially new growth and leaf undersides.
• Good airflow between trees. Do not crowd.
• Quarantine new trees for 2 weeks before adding to display.
• Clean tools between trees with rubbing alcohol.
• Healthy trees resist pests better — proper watering and feeding is the best defense.
Bonsai Tree Care Journal 9
Reference Bonsai Styles
Classical Bonsai Styles
The five foundational forms and their variations
Formal Upright (Chokkan)
Perfectly straight trunk, tapering from base to apex. Branches alternate left-right-back. The most challenging form. Best: Pine, Juniper, Maple.
Informal Upright (Moyogi)
Curving S-shaped trunk. Most popular style. Natural look. Branches on outside of curves. Best: Most species.
Slanting (Shakan)
Trunk leans at 60-80 degrees. Root mass anchors the leaning side. Suggests wind or cliff-side growth. Best: Pine, Juniper, Elm.
Cascade (Kengai)
Trunk grows downward below the pot rim, mimicking a tree on a cliff. Needs a deep pot. Best: Juniper, Pine, Cotoneaster.
Semi-Cascade (Han-Kengai)
Trunk extends horizontally then cascades but does not go below the pot base. Less dramatic than full cascade. Best: Juniper, Flowering species.
Literati (Bunjingi)
Tall slender trunk with minimal foliage at top. Elegant and sparse. Inspired by Chinese scholar paintings. Best: Pine, Juniper.
Broom (Hokidachi)
Straight trunk branching into a fan-shaped canopy at the top. Resembles a broom. Best: Zelkova, Elm, Maple.
Forest (Yose-ue)
Multiple trees planted together to create a miniature forest. Odd numbers (3, 5, 7) look most natural. Best: Maple, Elm, Larch.
Root-over-Rock (Ishitsuki)
Roots grow over and grip a rock, then descend into soil. Dramatic and ancient look. Best: Ficus, Maple, Juniper.
Windswept (Fukinagashi)
All branches grow in one direction, as if shaped by constant wind. Evokes struggle and survival. Best: Juniper, Pine.
These forms are guides, not rules. The best bonsai reflects the natural character of the tree and the vision of the artist. Study mature trees in nature for inspiration.

Bonsai Tree Care Journal 96
Seasonal Care My Year-Round Task Calendar
Seasonal Care Calendar
Plan and track recurring tasks by season
Spring
Summer
Fall
Winter
Season Key Tasks Done?
Spring Repot deciduous when buds swell. Structural pruning. Apply wire. Start feeding (low nitrogen first). Protect from late frost.
Spring Pine: decandle. Juniper: pinch growth. Check for aphids on new growth. Move outdoor trees to full sun.
Summer Water 1-2x daily in heat. Defoliate maples (mid-summer). Fertilize regularly. Shade sensitive species from afternoon sun.
Summer Watch for spider mites. Trim long shoots. Apply wire to summer growth. Tropicals: peak growing season, prune hard.
Fall Stop nitrogen fertilizer (switch to 0-10-10 for hardening). Remove wire before dormancy. Clean dead leaves.
Fall Light pruning only. Jin and shari work on junipers. Bring tropicals indoors. Prepare winter shelter for hardy trees.
Winter Structural pruning of bare deciduous branches. Major branch removal. Protect pots from freezing. Minimal watering.
Winter Plan next year's design changes. Order supplies (soil, wire, pots). Study and sketch. Inventory tool condition.
