Getting Started More Shine Press
How to Use This Journal
Every batch is a learning opportunity
Why Document Your Soap?
Soap making is both art and science. A single degree change in temperature, a different oil ratio, or a new fragrance oil can transform your results. Without records, you cannot reproduce your best batches or learn from the ones that failed. This journal turns every batch into accumulated expertise.
Tips for Better Soap Records
1. Weigh everything. Soap making requires precision. Use a digital scale accurate to 0.1 oz or 1 gram. Record actual weights, not just percentages. Volume measurements (cups, spoons) are not accurate enough.
2. Note temperatures. Record the temperature of your oils and lye solution at trace. Temperature affects saponification speed, color, and texture. Ideal range is typically 90-110°F for cold process.
3. Time your trace. Record how long it took to reach trace and what kind of trace (thin, medium, thick). This reveals how oils, fragrances, and temperatures affect your formula.
4. Track cure time and results. Test bars at 2, 4, and 6 weeks. Note hardness, lather quality, scent strength, and skin feel. A soap that seems harsh at 2 weeks may be perfect at 6.
5. Photograph every batch. Cut bars and photograph cross-sections. Record a reference photo number in your journal. Colors and patterns are hard to describe in words.
6. Record failures honestly. Ricing, seizing, acceleration, DOS (dreaded orange spots), and lye-heavy batches happen to everyone. Documenting the cause prevents repeating mistakes.

Soap Making Journal 4
Reference Lye Safety
Lye Safety Guidelines
Essential safety for working with sodium hydroxide
The Most Important Rule
Always add lye to water. Never add water to lye. Adding water to lye causes a violent exothermic reaction that can boil, splash, and cause severe chemical burns. Memorize: "Snow falls on the lake."
Personal Protection
1. Safety goggles (not glasses) at all times when handling lye. Lye in eyes causes permanent blindness.
2. Long rubber or nitrile gloves. Lye burns skin on contact.
3. Long sleeves, pants, and closed-toe shoes. Protect all skin.
4. Keep vinegar nearby for skin contact (neutralizes alkaline burn). Rinse with water first, then vinegar.
5. Work in a well-ventilated area. Lye fumes are harmful. Never lean over the lye solution.
Equipment
6. Use only stainless steel, heat-safe glass, or heavy plastic for lye. Never use aluminum (reacts violently) or copper.
7. Dedicated soap-making equipment only. Do not reuse pots, spoons, or containers for food.
8. Use an immersion blender dedicated to soap making. Clean all equipment thoroughly after use.
9. Digital scale accurate to 0.1 oz or 1 gram. Precision is non-negotiable.
Mixing Lye Solution
10. Weigh lye and water separately. Always add lye to water, never reverse.
11. Stir slowly with a heat-safe spatula. Solution reaches 200°F+ instantly.
12. Let solution cool to 90-110°F before mixing with oils. Track temperature.
13. Label and store lye containers clearly. Keep away from children and pets.
14. Clean spills immediately with paper towels, then wash with water and vinegar.

Soap Making Journal 6
Reference Curing & Testing
Curing Your Soap
Patience makes perfect bars
Why Cure?
Fresh soap contains excess water and the saponification process is not fully complete. Curing allows water to evaporate (making bars harder and longer-lasting) and lets the pH settle. A well-cured bar is milder, harder, and lathers better. Rush this step and you get soft, dissolving, harsh bars.
Timeline Water Loss What to Check
Week 1 ~5-8% Bars are still soft. Keep in mold first 24-48 hrs, then cut. Place on curing racks with airflow.
Week 2 ~10-15% Bars firming up. Test for hardness with thumbnail press. Should leave slight indent.
Week 4 ~15-20% Bars feel hard. Initial lather test. Scent should be noticeable. pH should test below 10.
Week 6 ~20-25% Standard cure complete for most recipes. Good lather, mild feel, lasting bar.
Week 8+ ~25-30% Extended cure for high-olive recipes. Even milder, denser lather. Bars last longest.
Curing Best Practices
FactorIdeal
AirflowOpen shelving or racks. Fans circulating air. No sealed containers.
HumidityLow (under 50% if possible). Use dehumidifier in damp climates.
TemperatureRoom temperature (65-75°F). Avoid extreme heat or cold.
SpacingBars not touching. At least 0.5 inch between for airflow.
LightAvoid direct sunlight (causes fading and DOS). Dark or dim storage.
TurningFlip bars every few days for even drying on all sides.
Testing Your Soap
TestWhat to Look For
Zap TestLick the soap. A "zap" or tingle = lye-heavy, not ready. No zap = safe to use.
pH StripWet soap surface, press pH strip. Readings 8-10 are normal for soap. Above 11 = uncured.
LatherWash hands. Check for bubbly vs creamy lather, how it rinses, skin feel after.
HardnessPress thumbnail into bar. Hard, slight give = good. Soft, crumbly = keep curing.
Soap Making Journal 7
Reference Troubleshooting
Common Soap Problems
Identify and fix batch issues
Problem Likely Cause Solution
Seize Fragrance oil reacts instantly. Temperature too high. FO not CP-safe. Work at lower temp. Test FO in small batch. Switch FO. Use hot process.
Acceleration FO/EO speeds trace. Stearic acid, hard butters. Temp too high. Lower oil/lye temps. Add FO at very light trace. Work fast.
Ricing FO forms rice-like lumps in batter. Common with floral FOs. Stick blend vigorously to incorporate. May incorporate or need straining.
DOS (Orange Spots) Oil rancidity. Old oils, high superfat, humidity during cure. Use fresh oils. Add ROE or vitamin E. Lower superfat. Cure in low humidity.
Lye Heavy Mismeasured lye. Wrong SAP values. Scale error. Insufficient cure. Always verify with lye calculator. Zap test before use. Recalculate recipe.
Soda Ash Lye reacts with air CO2 on surface. Uncovered during saponification. Spray with rubbing alcohol after pour. Cover with plastic wrap. Steam or wash off.
Gel Partial Uneven temperature during saponification. Center gels, edges do not. Insulate mold fully. Force gel phase (oven process). Or embrace ungelled look.
Cracking High lye concentration. Too much coconut oil. Temperature shock. Use more water. Reduce coconut oil. Control temperatures.
Color Fade UV light exposure. Unstable colorant. High pH destroying pigment. Use stable pigments (oxides, micas). Store dark. Avoid plant colorants.
No Scent Insufficient EO/FO. Citrus oils fading. Volatile oils evaporating. Increase usage rate. Use stable oils (patchouli, litsea). Seal cure area.
Soft Bars High water. High soft oil ratio. Not fully cured. High superfat. Cure longer. Reduce water. Increase hard oils (palm, cocoa butter).
Glycerin Rivers Temperature too high during gel. High glycerin in recipe. Lower lye concentration. Reduce gel temp. Prevent overheating.
Pro Tip: When a batch fails, do not throw it away. Document the problem, let it cure fully, then zap-test. Many "failed" batches become usable after a longer cure. Lye-heavy soap can be rebatched (hot process) or used for laundry.
