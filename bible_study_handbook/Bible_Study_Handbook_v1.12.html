Getting Started
How to Use This Handbook

This handbook is a 52-week journey through the entire Bible, from Genesis to Revelation. Each week covers a major passage, theme, or biblical figure. The curriculum is organized into 10 epochs that trace God's redemptive story from creation to new creation.

Weekly Structure
Passage: The scripture reading for the week. Look it up in your own Bible.
Theme: A teaching overview to guide your study and reflection.
Key Verse: One verse to memorize or meditate on throughout the week.
Big Idea: The one-sentence takeaway to carry with you.
Questions: Four reflection prompts for personal study or group discussion.
Christ Connection: How this passage points to Jesus.
Action Step: One practical thing to do this week.
Journal Page
Each week includes a full journaling page with space to write. The layout rotates between
three study methods so your experience stays fresh all year long:

Layout A — SOAP Method: Write your own observation, application, and prayer.
Layout B — Verse Mapping: Break down keywords, cross-references, and meaning.
Layout C — Inductive Study: Observe, interpret, and apply the text directly.
Tips
Set aside a regular time each day (even 15 minutes).
Use the questions for journaling or small group discussion.
Don't rush. Some weeks may take longer than seven days — that's okay.
Pray before you read. Ask the Holy Spirit to illuminate the text.
This handbook uses only verse references — no translated Scripture text — so you can use it alongside any Bible translation you prefer.

WEEK 01 Epoch 1 · Beginnings Week 1 of 52
Creation
God as Artist and Order-Maker
Scripture: Genesis 1:1-2:3
Theme & Teaching
Before there was anything, there was Someone. Genesis opens not with an argument but with a declaration: God speaks, and reality obeys. Notice the rhythm — six days of forming and filling, each building on the last. Light into darkness, seas from sky, life teeming in every niche. Women are not an afterthought; both male and female bear God's image (1:27), commissioned together to steward creation.
Genesis 1:1
The first verse of Scripture leaves no room for a God who is created. He is the uncreated Creator. Everything else has a beginning; He does not.
Big Idea
You are not an accident in a random universe. You are the deliberate artwork of a God who ordered chaos into beauty.
Reflection Questions
1.What does it mean that you are made in God's image? How does that change how you see yourself?
2.Where in your life do you see the remnants of God's original 'very good' design, even in a broken world?
3.God worked six days and rested on the seventh. What would it look like to embrace Sabbath rest this week?
4.How does knowing God as Creator affect your response when life feels chaotic or out of control?
Christ Connection
John 1:1-3 reveals that Jesus — the Word — was present and active at creation. Colossians 1:16 says all things were created through Him and for Him. The Creator became part of creation to redeem it.
Action Step
Take a walk outside this week. As you observe creation, thank God specifically for five things He made. Write them here.

WEEK 02 Epoch 1 · Beginnings Week 2 of 52
The Fall
Where Everything Broke
Scripture: Genesis 3:1-24
Theme & Teaching
One conversation changed everything. The serpent's strategy hasn't changed in thousands of years: he questions God's word ('Did God really say?'), denies God's consequences ('You will not surely die'), and reinterprets God's motive. Eve was deceived; Adam was present and silent. Both ate. Both hid. But even in judgment, God provides — clothing them, and promising that the woman's seed would crush the serpent's head (3:15).
Genesis 3:15
This is the first promise of the gospel — protoevangelion. A descendant of the woman will defeat the serpent. The war is announced, but so is the victory.
Big Idea
Sin fractures everything — but even as God pronounces consequences, He plants the first promise of redemption.
Reflection Questions
1.The serpent questioned God's goodness. Where do you hear that same whisper in your own life?
2.Adam blamed Eve, Eve blamed the serpent. What does blame reveal about the human heart?
3.God came looking: 'Where are you?' He knew where they were — the question was for them. Where are you hiding from God right now?
4.How does Genesis 3:15 — God's first promise — change the way you read the rest of the Bible?
Christ Connection
Jesus is the 'seed of the woman' who crushes the serpent's head at the cross (Romans 16:20; Revelation 12). Where Adam and Eve failed, Christ succeeded.
Action Step
Identify one area where you've believed 'Did God really say?' Write down what God actually says about it in His Word.

Cain, Abel & The Flood
When Violence Spread
Scripture: Genesis 4:1-16; 6:5-9:17
Theme & Teaching
Sin didn't stay in the garden. By the next generation, brother murders brother. By Noah's time, 'every intention of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually' (6:5). The flood is God's judgment on unbearable evil — but also His mercy: He preserves one family, one remnant, and makes a covenant marked by the rainbow.
Genesis 6:8
'But Noah found favor in the eyes of the Lord.' In a world drowning in violence, one man walked with God. Favor is not earned; it's received by grace.
Big Idea
Sin escalates rapidly when left unchecked — but God always preserves a faithful remnant.
Reflection Questions
1.God warned Cain: 'Sin is crouching at the door.' What sin is crouching at your door?
2.Noah built an ark on dry land for 100+ years while people mocked. What has God asked you to build that others don't understand?
3.The rainbow is a covenant sign — God's bow laid down. What 'signs' of God's faithfulness has He placed in your life?
4.How does the flood narrative change your view of both God's justice and His mercy?
Christ Connection
Jesus compared the days of Noah to His return (Matthew 24:37-39). The ark is a picture of Christ — the one place of salvation from the judgment to come (1 Peter 3:20-21).
Action Step
Write a prayer of confession for one area where sin has been 'crouching.' Then thank God for His covenant faithfulness.

WEEK 04 Epoch 1 · Beginnings Week 4 of 52
Babel & Nations
Pride, Scattering, and God's Global Heart
Scripture: Genesis 10:1-11:9
Theme & Teaching
At Babel, humanity united for the wrong reason: 'Let us make a name for ourselves.' They built a tower to heaven — not to reach God, but to replace Him. God's response is both judgment and mercy: He confuses their languages and scatters them. But Acts 17:26-27 reveals that God determined the boundaries of every nation 'so that they should seek Him.' The scattering was preparation for the gathering.
Genesis 11:4
'Let us make a name for ourselves' — the heartbeat of all human pride. Contrast with Abram, to whom God says, 'I will make your name great.'
Big Idea
Human ambition builds towers; God's grace builds nations and writes a story of redemption across every people.
Reflection Questions
1.Where in your life are you trying to 'make a name for yourself' rather than glorify God's name?
2.God scattered the nations — but Pentecost begins gathering them back. How does this change your view of cultural diversity?
3.The table of nations (Gen 10) shows God knows every people group. How does this shape your heart for missions?
4.What 'tower' have you been building that God may need to scatter?
Christ Connection
At Pentecost (Acts 2), the curse of Babel is reversed — every nation hears the gospel in their own language. Revelation 7:9 shows the final result: people from every tribe worshipping together.
Action Step
Pray for one unreached people group this week. Ask your church or research nations that have yet to hear the Gospel, and choose one to intercede for.

WEEK 05 Epoch 2 · The Patriarchs Week 5 of 52
Abraham
Faith That Stumbled and Still Stood
Scripture: Genesis 12:1-9; 15:1-21; 22:1-19
Theme & Teaching
God called a 75-year-old man with no children and promised him descendants as numerous as the stars. Abraham believed — and also lied, laughed, and tried to help God's plan along with Hagar. His faith was real but imperfect. Genesis 15:6 is the verse that changes everything: 'He believed the Lord, and He counted it to him as righteousness.' Paul quotes this in Romans 4 to prove we are saved by faith, not works.
Genesis 15:6
This is the first clear statement of justification by faith in the Bible. Abraham wasn't perfect — he was credited as righteous because he believed God's promise.
Big Idea
Faith is not flawlessness — it is trusting God's promise even when your circumstances scream otherwise.
Reflection Questions
1.Abraham left everything familiar at 75. What is God asking you to leave behind to follow Him?
2.Abraham tried to 'help' God's promise by having Ishmael. Where have you taken matters into your own hands instead of waiting?
3.Genesis 22 — the binding of Isaac — is the hardest chapter. What does it reveal about God's provision?
4.Paul says Abraham is the 'father of all who believe' (Romans 4:16). What does it mean to have Abraham's kind of faith today?
Christ Connection
God provided a ram in place of Isaac on Mount Moriah. 2,000 years later, on that same mountain range, God provided His own Son as the substitute sacrifice. Jesus is the ultimate 'Lamb of God.'
Action Step
Write down one promise from God's Word that feels impossible right now. Commit to trusting it for 30 days.

WEEK 06 Epoch 2 · The Patriarchs Week 6 of 52
Sarah & Hagar
Two Women, One Promise, One God Who Sees
Scripture: Genesis 16:1-16; 18:1-15; 21:8-21
Theme & Teaching
This is one of the most heartbreakingly real stories in Scripture. Sarah, barren for decades, gives her servant Hagar to Abraham — a culturally accepted but spiritually disastrous solution. Hagar conceives, and Sarah's jealousy erupts. Hagar runs into the wilderness, pregnant and alone. And there, God meets her. She names Him 'El Roi' — 'The God who sees me.' Paul uses their story as an allegory of law vs. grace in Galatians 4.
Genesis 16:13
'You are a God of seeing... Have I indeed seen Him who looks after me?' Hagar — an Egyptian slave, an outsider — is the only person in Scripture to give God a name.
Big Idea
God sees the women the world overlooks. He hears the cry of the marginalized, and His promise comes through grace, not human effort.
Reflection Questions
1.Hagar was alone, rejected, and pregnant in the desert — and God found her. When have you felt most unseen?
2.Sarah's impatience led to Ishmael. What happens when we force God's timing?
3.Galatians 4 says Hagar represents the law and Sarah represents grace. Which are you living under?
4.Both women were flawed. How does God's grace work through imperfect, even competing, women?
Christ Connection
Paul uses this story to show that we are 'children of the free woman' — born by the Spirit, not by human effort (Galatians 4:28-31). Jesus makes us children of promise.
Action Step
Write 'El Roi — The God Who Sees Me' somewhere visible this week. Each time you see it, remember He sees you.

WEEK 07 Epoch 2 · The Patriarchs Week 7 of 52
Jacob & Esau
The Deceiver Meets God
Scripture: Genesis 25:19-34; 27:1-45; 28:10-22; 32:22-32
Theme & Teaching
Jacob's name means 'heel-grabber' — a trickster from birth. He swindled his brother's birthright, deceived his blind father, and spent 20 years running. But at Bethel, sleeping on a stone, he dreams of a staircase between heaven and earth. At the Jabbok River, he wrestles with God Himself and walks away limping — but with a new name: Israel. God doesn't just reform Jacob; He re-names him.
Genesis 32:28
'Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel.' God changes identities, not just behaviors. The new name comes through the struggle, not around it.
Big Idea
God doesn't just want to improve you — He wants to transform your identity. Sometimes it takes a wrestling match.
Reflection Questions
1.Jacob grasped for the blessing through deception. Where are you grasping for what God wants to give freely?
2.At Bethel, God appeared to Jacob in a dream. How does God pursue you even in your lowest moments?
3.Jacob wrestled all night and said, 'I will not let you go unless you bless me.' What would it look like to wrestle with God like that?
4.Jacob walked with a limp after his encounter. How have your struggles left a permanent mark — and how is that a sign of grace?
Christ Connection
Jesus told Nathanael, 'You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man' (John 1:51) — He is the true staircase between heaven and earth.
Action Step
What 'name' have you been carrying that doesn't match who God says you are? Write the old name, cross it out, and write the identity God gives you in Christ.

WEEK 08 Epoch 2 · The Patriarchs Week 8 of 52
Joseph
Sovereignty Woven Through Suffering
Scripture: Genesis 37:1-36; 39:1-23; 45:1-15; 50:15-21
Theme & Teaching
Joseph's life reads like a novel: favored son, sold by brothers, falsely accused, forgotten in prison, raised to power in Egypt. But the narrative keeps pulling back the curtain to show God's invisible hand. Genesis 50:20 is the thesis: 'You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good.' This isn't just Joseph being forgiving — it's a theological claim that God was actively working inside human evil to save many lives.
Genesis 50:20
The Hebrew word 'meant' is the same for both the brothers' evil and God's good. Same events, different intentions. God doesn't cause evil — but He weaves it into His redemptive purposes.
Big Idea
What others mean for destruction, God can mean for salvation. Your suffering is not wasted — it is being woven.
Reflection Questions
1.Joseph resisted Potiphar's wife at great cost. What does integrity look like when doing the right thing leads to punishment?
2.Joseph spent 13 years as a slave and prisoner. What do you do when you are in the 'waiting room' of God's plan?
3.How would you respond if the people who hurt you most were standing in front of you, helpless? What does Joseph's model teach?
4.Genesis 50:20 requires trusting God's sovereignty in your deepest pain. Can you say 'God meant it for good' about something done to you?
Christ Connection
Joseph is one of the clearest Old Testament types of Christ: the beloved son, rejected by his brothers, sold for silver, falsely accused, exalted to the right hand of the ruler, and the savior who says, 'Come to me.'
Action Step
Write down one painful experience. Under it, write: 'God is able to use even this.' Pray for eyes to see how He is already working.

WEEK 09 Epoch 3 · Exodus & Conquest Week 9 of 52
Moses & The Burning Bush
When God Calls the Reluctant
Scripture: Exodus 2:1-10; 3:1-4:17
Theme & Teaching
A Hebrew baby saved by an Egyptian princess, raised in privilege, exiled as a murderer, tending sheep in obscurity — and then a bush that won't stop burning. God reveals His name as 'I AM' (Yahweh). Moses offers five excuses; God answers each one. The call of God always exposes our insufficiency in order to reveal His sufficiency. Consider also the women who shaped Moses' early life: the midwives Shiphrah and Puah who defied Pharaoh, Jochebed his mother who hid him, Miriam his sister who watched, and Pharaoh's daughter who adopted him.
Exodus 3:14
'I AM WHO I AM.' God doesn't define Himself by relationship to anything else. He simply IS. Self-existent, self-sufficient, uncaused.
Big Idea
God doesn't call the qualified; He qualifies the called. Your inadequacy is exactly where His power shows up.
Reflection Questions
1.Five women — Shiphrah, Puah, Jochebed, Miriam, Pharaoh's daughter — shaped Moses' survival. Who are the women who shaped your faith?
2.Moses had five excuses for God. What is your favorite excuse when God calls you to something difficult?
3.God said 'I AM.' What does it mean that God is eternally present-tense — never past, never future?
4.Moses spent 40 years in the wilderness before the burning bush. What if your current 'wilderness' is actually preparation?
Christ Connection
Jesus invoked 'I AM' seven times in John's Gospel ('I am the bread of life,' 'I am the good shepherd,' etc.) — claiming the divine name for Himself (John 8:58).
Action Step
Write down your top excuse for why God can't use you. Now write 'I AM sent me' next to it.

WEEK 10 Epoch 3 · Exodus & Conquest Week 10 of 52
The Ten Plagues
God vs. The Gods of Egypt
Scripture: Exodus 7:1-12:42
Theme & Teaching
The plagues were not random punishments — each one targeted a specific Egyptian deity, showing that Yahweh is supreme over every false god. The Nile turned to blood (against Hapi). Darkness (against Ra). The final plague — the death of the firstborn — broke Pharaoh's resistance and established the Passover: a lamb without blemish, blood on the doorposts, death passing over.
Exodus 12:13

'But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control; against such things there is no law.' Fruit grows, it is not produced by effort.
Big Idea
Fruit is evidence of connection. You don't produce character by trying harder — you abide, and fruit follows.
Reflection Questions
1.Which 'fruit' is most evident in your life right now? Which is most absent?
2.Jesus said 'apart from Me you can do nothing' (John 15:5). What would it look like to 'abide' practically this week?
3.Paul contrasts the 'works of the flesh' with the 'fruit of the Spirit.' How can you tell which is operating in a given moment?
4.Fruit takes time. Are you patient with your own spiritual growth, or do you demand instant results?
Christ Connection
Jesus is the vine; we are the branches. The Spirit produces in us what Christ embodied perfectly. 'Walk by the Spirit, and you will not gratify the desires of the flesh' (Galatians 5:16).
Action Step
Each morning this week, pray: 'Holy Spirit, produce Your fruit in me today.' At night, review: which fruit showed up? Which was missing?

Women in the Early Church
Co-Laborers in the Gospel
Scripture: Romans 16:1-7; Acts 18:24-28; Philippians 4:2-3
Theme & Teaching
Phoebe, a deacon, carried Paul's letter to Rome. Priscilla, with her husband Aquila, taught Apollos — one of the most gifted preachers of the early church. Junia is recognized by Paul as outstanding among the apostles (Romans 16:7). Lydia, a businesswoman, hosted the first European church. Euodia and Syntyche 'labored side by side' with Paul in the gospel. The early church was built on women's leadership, hospitality, teaching, and financial support.
Romans 16:1-2
'I commend to you our sister Phoebe, a servant [deacon] of the church at Cenchreae, that you may welcome her in the Lord in a way worthy of the saints, and help her in whatever she may need from you, for she has been a patron of many, and of myself as well.' Phoebe was a leader, a patron, and the carrier of Romans.
Big Idea
Women were not peripheral to the early church — they were foundational. Your gifts are needed, not optional.
Reflection Questions
1.Phoebe carried Romans — arguably the most important theological text in history. What does it mean that a woman was trusted with this?
2.Priscilla taught Apollos. How does this expand your understanding of women's roles in teaching and leadership?
3.Lydia's hospitality birthed a church (Acts 16). How might God use your home, resources, or influence for His kingdom?
4.Paul names 10 women in Romans 16. Why has church history often minimized their contributions?
Christ Connection
In Christ, 'there is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus' (Galatians 3:28). The Spirit gifts and calls without distinction.
Action Step
Research one woman from church history you've never heard of. Write down what you learn and how her story challenges or encourages you.

James & Peter: Faith That Works
Faith Under Fire
Scripture: James 1:1-12; 2:14-26; 1 Peter 1:3-9
Theme & Teaching
James, Jesus' brother, writes the most practical book in the New Testament: 'Faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead' (2:17). Not faith vs. works, but faith that works. Peter writes to suffering believers: 'In this you rejoice, though now for a little while... you have been grieved by various trials, so that the tested genuineness of your faith... may be found to result in praise and glory' (1 Peter 1:6-7).
James 1:2-4
'Count it all joy, my brothers, when you meet trials of various kinds, for you know that the testing of your faith produces steadfastness.' Joy is not the trial but the outcome it produces.
Big Idea
Real faith is tested faith. What trials reveal is the genuine quality of what you believe.
Reflection Questions
1.James says faith without works is dead. How do you know if your faith is living or dead?
2.'Count it all joy' in trials. This isn't denial — it's perspective. How does knowing the outcome (steadfastness, completeness) change how you face hardship?
3.Peter writes to 'elect exiles' — people whose true home is elsewhere. How does this identity help you navigate suffering?
4.What trial right now is testing the genuineness of your faith?
Christ Connection
Both James and Peter point to Christ as the anchor. James calls Him 'the Lord of glory' (2:1). Peter says we are 'guarded through faith for a salvation ready to be revealed' (1:5). He is the foundation that holds when everything shakes.
Action Step
Identify your current trial. Instead of asking 'Why?', ask: 'What steadfastness is God developing in me through this?'

Revelation: Letters to the Churches
The One Who Has an Ear
Scripture: Revelation 1:1-20; 2:1-3:22
Theme & Teaching
Revelation begins not with dragons and beasts but with a vision of the risen Christ among lampstands — the churches. Seven letters, each personal: 'I know your works.' Some are commended, some warned, all invited: 'He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the churches.' The famous 'Behold, I stand at the door and knock' (3:20) was written to a complacent church, not to unbelievers.
Revelation 3:20
'Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and eat with him, and he with Me.' Christ wants intimacy, not just attendance.
Big Idea
Christ walks among His churches. He sees everything — your perseverance, your compromise, your first love lost — and He invites you back.
Reflection Questions
1.Which of the seven churches sounds most like you? (Ephesus = lost first love; Smyrna = suffering; Pergamum = compromising; Thyatira = tolerating false teaching; Sardis = dead; Philadelphia = faithful; Laodicea = lukewarm)
2.'I know your works.' If Christ wrote a letter to you, what would He commend? What would He warn?
3.'To the one who conquers...' — each letter promises a reward. Which promise captures your heart?
4.Laodicea was 'lukewarm' — neither hot nor cold. Where have you settled for comfortable Christianity?
Christ Connection
Revelation opens with a vision of Christ — 'the firstborn of the dead, and the ruler of kings on earth' (1:5). He is the one who 'loves us and has freed us from our sins by His blood' (1:5). Everything else in Revelation flows from this vision.
Action Step
Write a letter to your own soul as if Christ were dictating it. Begin: 'I know your works...' Be honest. Then write His invitation.

Worthy Is the Lamb
Scripture: Revelation 4:1-5:14; 7:9-17
Theme & Teaching
John sees a door in heaven. On the throne sits One whose appearance is like jasper and carnelian. A rainbow surrounds the throne. Twenty-four elders fall down and cast their crowns. Revelation 5:9-10
'Worthy are You to take the scroll and open its seals, for You were slain, and by Your blood You ransomed people for God from every tribe and language and people and nation.' Worthiness is defined by sacrifice.
Big Idea
The center of the universe is a throne. And on that throne is a slaughtered Lamb who reigns through self-giving love.
Reflection Questions
1.The Lamb 'standing as though slain' is a paradox: the victim is the victor. How does this reframe your understanding of power?
2.Every creature will eventually worship (5:13). How does knowing the end change how you live now?
3.Revelation 7:9 — 'a great multitude... from every nation' — shows God's plan fulfilled. How diverse will heaven be? How should that shape your church now?
4.'The One seated on the throne will shelter them with His presence' (7:15). What does it mean to be sheltered by God?
Christ Connection
The Lamb is Jesus — the same Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world (John 1:29). His sacrifice on the cross is the event that unlocks all of history. He is simultaneously the victim and the victor, the slain one and the reigning King.
Action Step

Revelation: New Creation
He Makes All Things New
Scripture: Revelation 21:1-22:21
Theme & Teaching
The Bible ends where it began — in a garden, with a tree, with God present. But this garden is a city: the New Jerusalem, coming down from heaven. 'Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be His people, and God Himself will be with them as their God. He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more' (21:3-4). The promise of Genesis 3:15 — crushed serpent, restored creation — is finally fulfilled. 'Surely I am coming soon.' Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!
Revelation 21:5
'And He who was seated on the throne said, "Behold, I am making all things new."' Not 'all new things' — all things new. Redemption, not replacement. God restores what was broken.
Big Idea
The story doesn't end with destruction — it ends with renewal. God doesn't throw away creation; He redeems it. And He invites you to live now in the light of then.
Reflection Questions
1.'No more death, mourning, crying, or pain' (21:4). Which of these do you most long for? What loss would He wipe away?
2.'I am making all things new' — not replacing, renewing. What in your life needs not replacement but redemption?
3.The New Jerusalem has gates that 'will never be shut' (21:25). What does perfect, fearless openness feel like?
4.The Bible's last prayer is 'Come, Lord Jesus!' (22:20). Do you live with that longing? What would change if you did?
Christ Connection
The One seated on the throne declares 'Behold, I am making all things new' (Rev 21:5) — and Christ is the one who fulfills this promise. His first coming inaugurated the new creation; His return will complete it. 'Surely I am coming soon' (22:20). The Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end, the first and the last.
Action Step
Write down: 'He is making all things new.' Then answer: What in my life needs His renewing touch? Surrender it. End your study with the Bible's last prayer: 'Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!'

For Small Groups
Group Study Guide

This handbook works beautifully for group study. Whether you meet weekly in homes, at church, or online, here is a suggested format for a 60–90 minute gathering.

Before You Meet
Each member reads the week's Scripture passage and completes the journal page independently. Come prepared to share one insight or question.
Suggested Meeting Format
1. Open in Prayer (5 min) — Invite the Holy Spirit to guide your discussion.
2. Read the Passage Aloud (10 min) — Take turns reading. Try different translations.
3. Discuss the Big Idea (10 min) — What stood out? What surprised you?
4. Work Through the Questions (25 min) — Use the four reflection questions from the study page.
5. Share Christ Connections (10 min) — How does this passage point to Jesus?
6. Commit to Action Steps (10 min) — Each person shares their action step for the week.
7. Close in Prayer (10 min) — Pray for each other by name.
Facilitator Tips
Let silence happen — people need time to think before sharing.
There are no wrong answers in personal reflection. Honor every perspective.
If debate arises on interpretation, point back to the text: "What does the Scripture actually say?"
Keep Christ at the center. Every passage ultimately points to Him.
