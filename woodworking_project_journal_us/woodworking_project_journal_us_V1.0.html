How to Use This Journal
Measure twice, document once
Why Document Your Builds?
Every project teaches lessons. A journal captures dimensions that worked, joints that held, and finishes that looked beautiful. When you revisit a project months later, your notes save you from repeating mistakes and help you refine techniques. Your journal becomes a personal reference library of hard-won woodworking knowledge.
Tips for Better Documentation
1. Sketch before you cut. Draw the project with dimensions before heading to the shop. A dimensioned sketch catches design flaws and generates your cut list automatically.
2. Record the actual dimensions. Plans change in the shop. Write down the real measurements you used, not just the plan dimensions. This is how reproductions get easier.
3. Note wood movement. Record the wood species, grain orientation, and how the piece behaved over seasons. Wood moves — your joints should account for it.
4. Document finish schedules. Write down every step of your finishing process, including products, number of coats, sanding grits, and drying times. Finish results are hard to reproduce from memory.
5. Track time and cost honestly. Record actual material costs and hours spent. This transforms future estimates from guesswork into data. You will be surprised what projects actually cost.
6. Photograph the finished piece. Take photos from multiple angles in good light. Store a reference photo number in your journal entry.

Reference Joinery Methods
Common Woodworking Joints
From simple to refined
Butt Joint
Simplest joint. Two pieces meet at 90°. Weak without fasteners. Use screws, dowels, or biscuits for strength. Good for quick utility projects.
Miter Joint
Two pieces cut at 45° forming a 90° corner. Hides end grain. Elegant but weak without reinforcement. Reinforce with splines or biscuits.
Dado
A square-bottomed groove cut across the grain. Excellent for shelves and cabinet construction. Strong mechanical connection. Cut with dado stack or router.
Rabbet
A groove cut along the edge of a board. Used for cabinet backs, drawer construction, and joinery. Provides good glue surface and alignment.
Dado + Rabbet
Combines both for casework corners. Self-aligning and strong. Standard for cabinet and bookshelf construction.
Mortise & Tenon
A rectangular projection (tenon) fits into a matching hole (mortise). The gold standard for frame construction. Very strong. Used in chairs, tables, doors.
Half-Blind Dovetail
Interlocking pins and tails, hidden from the front. Premium drawer joint. Extremely strong. Machine-cut or hand-cut. Shows craftsmanship.
Through Dovetail
Visible interlocking pins and tails on both faces. The strongest and most decorative joint. Used for boxes, chests, and fine casework. Hand-cut = master level.
Box Joint (Finger)
Rectangular interlocking fingers. Easier to cut than dovetails. Strong glue surface. Great for boxes and drawers. Cut on table saw or router.
Biscuit Joint
Oval compressed wood wafers (biscuits) fit into matching slots. Great for alignment in panel glue-ups and miter joints. Quick with a biscuit joiner.
Pocket Hole
Angled holes drilled with a jig. Screws pull pieces together. Fast and strong. Great for face frames and beginner-friendly joinery.
Tongue & Groove
A projecting tongue on one board fits into a groove on another. Used for flooring, paneling, and tabletops. Self-aligning and hides gaps.

Reference Finishing Guide
Finishing Options
Choose the right finish for each project
Finish Type Durab. Ease Application & Notes
Oil (Linseed/Tung) Low Easy Penetrating. Brings out grain, natural feel. Apply with rag, wipe off excess. Multiple coats. Food-safe when cured. Best for decorative pieces.
Oil + Wax Low Easy Oil finish followed by paste wax. Soft satin sheen. Beautiful on fine furniture. Reapply wax annually.
Shellac Med Med Quick-drying natural resin. French polish for high gloss. Sealer coat under other finishes. Not water/heat resistant. Food-safe (de-waxed).
Polyurethane High Med Durable clear coat. Water-based (clear, low odor) or oil-based (amber, tougher). Tabletops, floors. 3+ coats recommended.
Varnish High Med Durable and weather-resistant. Spar varnish for outdoor projects. Slower drying than poly. Brush or wipe on.
Lacquer High Hard Fast-drying professional finish. Spray application. Multiple thin coats melt into each other. Beautiful depth. Requires spray equipment.
Water-Based Med Easy Cleanup with water. Low odor. Non-yellowing. Good for painted projects and light woods. Less durable than oil-based.
Paint Med Easy Primer + 2 coats. Water-based latex (easy cleanup) or oil-based enamel (tougher). Use on poplar or MDF for painted furniture.
Danish Oil Med Easy Blend of oil and varnish. Easy wipe-on application. Some build plus grain enhancement. Great all-purpose finish for beginners.

Reference Shop Safety
Workshop Safety Rules
Protect yourself and your tools
The Number One Rule
Never put your fingers in the path of a blade. If an operation feels unsafe, it is. Stop, reassess, use a push stick, jig, or clamp. There is always a safer way to make any cut.
Personal Protection
1. Safety glasses always. No exceptions. Wood chips, sawdust, and broken bits fly in any direction.
2. Hearing protection for power tools. Table saws, routers, and planers cause permanent hearing damage over time.
3. Dust mask or respirator when sanding or cutting MDF. Fine dust causes serious lung damage. Connect dust collection where possible.
4. No loose clothing, jewelry, or long hair near spinning tools. Roll up sleeves, remove rings.
5. Closed-toe shoes. Steel-toe boots for heavy work.
Power Tool Safety
6. Table saw: Use the blade guard, riving knife, and anti-kickback pawls. Learn to recognize and avoid kickback.
7. Always use push sticks for narrow or short cuts. Keep fingers at least 6 inches from any blade.
8. Never freehand cut on a table saw. Use the fence or miter gauge. Never use both at once.
9. Unplug tools before changing blades or bits. Wait for blades to stop spinning completely.
10. Keep blades and bits sharp. Dull tools require more force, increasing risk of slips and kickback.
11. Keep a clean floor. Sawdust and offcuts cause slips. Sweep between operations.
Finishing Safety
12. Work in a well-ventilated area for solvent-based finishes. Use a vapor respirator for spraying.
13. No open flames near solvents. Oil-soaked rags can spontaneously combust — store in water or spread flat to dry.
14. Store chemicals properly, away from heat sources and out of reach of children.
