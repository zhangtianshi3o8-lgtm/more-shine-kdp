How to Use This Journal
Make every glass a learning experience
Why Keep a Wine Journal?
The difference between drinking wine and understanding wine is attention. A tasting journal helps you discover patterns — which varietals you gravitate toward, how oak and terroir shape flavor, what regions deliver the character you love. Over time, your journal becomes your personal wine roadmap.
The Five S's of Wine Tasting
1. See. Hold your glass at a 45-degree angle over a white surface. Note the color, depth, and clarity. Young reds are purple and opaque; older wines show brick-orange at the rim. White wines deepen from pale straw to gold with age.
2. Swirl. Give the glass a few gentle rotations on the table. This exposes the wine to air and releases its aromas. The tears (or legs) that drip down the glass hint at the wine's alcohol and body.
3. Sniff. Lower your nose into the glass and take short, gentle sniffs. Your nose detects far more than your palate ever will. Try to identify fruit, floral, spice, and earth notes. With practice, the aromas become vivid and specific.
4. Sip. Take a small sip and let it coat your tongue. Move it around your mouth. Note the sweetness, acidity, tannins, body, and flavor intensity. The texture is just as important as the taste.
5. Savor. Pay attention to the finish — the flavors and sensations that linger after you swallow. A long, evolving finish is the hallmark of a quality wine. Note how long it lasts and what it reveals.
Pro Tip: Always taste wine at the right temperature. Reds are best slightly below room temperature (60–65°F). Whites and rosés should be chilled but not ice-cold (45–50°F). Temperature dramatically affects how aromas and flavors come across.

Reference Wine Types Guide
Wine Types Guide
Every style has a story and a flavor profile
Red Wine
Made from dark-skinned grapes fermented with the grape skins, giving it color, tannin, and body. Ranges from light and elegant Pinot Noir to bold and structured Cabernet Sauvignon.
Best for: Pairing with red meat, hearty pasta, aging
White Wine
Made from green or dark-skinned grapes pressed immediately to avoid skin contact. From crisp, mineral Sauvignon Blanc to rich, buttery Chardonnay.
Best for: Seafood, poultry, sipping chilled
Rosé
Made from red grapes with limited skin contact (a few hours), resulting in a pink color. Fresh, vibrant, and food-friendly. Can be dry or slightly sweet.
Best for: Summer sipping, salads, light appetizers
Sparkling Wine
Wine with significant carbonation from a secondary fermentation. Methods include traditional bottle fermentation and tank methods. Ranges from bone-dry to sweet.
Best for: Celebrations, oysters, brunch
Dessert Wine
Sweet wines made from late-harvest, frozen, or botrytis-affected grapes. Concentrated, luscious, and complex. Includes late harvest and ice wine styles.
Best for: Pairing with dessert, blue cheese, foie gras
Fortified Wine
Wine fortified with a distilled spirit (brandy). Includes Port, Sherry, and Madeira. Rich, complex, and higher in alcohol (17–22% ABV).
Best for: After-dinner sipping, pairings with strong cheese
Orange Wine
White wine made with extended skin contact, like a red wine process. Amber color with tannic structure and unusual flavor profile. An ancient winemaking style.
Best for: Adventurous drinkers, pairing with bold flavors
Natural Wine
Wine made with minimal intervention — native yeast, little or no sulfur, no additives. Unfiltered and unpredictable. Growing in popularity among wine enthusiasts.
Best for: Curious palates, exploring new flavors

Reference Wine Regions of the World
Wine Regions Guide
Where your wine comes from shapes what it tastes like
France — Bordeaux
The world's largest fine wine region, famous for Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot blends. Left Bank is Cabernet-dominant; Right Bank favors Merlot. Structured, age-worthy reds.
France — Burgundy
The spiritual home of Pinot Noir and Chardonnay. Tiny, fragmented vineyards produce some of the world's most sought-after wines. Elegant, terroir-driven, and expensive.
France — Champagne
The birthplace of sparkling wine made by the traditional method. Only three grapes: Chardonnay, Pinot Noir, and Pinot Meunier. Bubbles, elegance, and prestige.
France — Rhône Valley
Syrah rules the north (bold, peppery, dark); Grenache leads the south (ripe, spicy, generous). Outstanding value and character at every level.
Italy — Tuscany
Home of Sangiovese-based wines: Chianti, Brunello, and Super Tuscans. Bright acidity, cherry fruit, and earthy undertones. Food-friendly and classic.
Italy — Piedmont
Nebbiolo country — Barolo and Barbaresco are among Italy's most prestigious reds. Tar and roses, high tannin, and extraordinary aging potential.
Spain — Rioja
Tempranillo-based reds aged in American and French oak. Known for vanilla, leather, and dried fruit notes. Classified by aging: Crianza, Reserva, Gran Reserva.
Germany — Mosel
The world's premier Riesling region. Steep slate slopes along the river produce wines with electric acidity, delicate fruit, and a distinctive mineral edge.
USA — California
America's largest wine state. Napa Valley is famous for world-class Cabernet Sauvignon; Sonoma offers diversity from Pinot Noir to Zinfandel. Bold, ripe, and fruit-forward.
USA — Oregon
Willamette Valley is Pinot Noir paradise — cool climate, elegant wines with bright acidity. Often compared to Burgundy in style. Also excellent Pinot Gris and Chardonnay.
Australia — Barossa Valley
Old-vine Shiraz with rich, concentrated dark fruit, spice, and soft tannins. Also outstanding Grenache and Riesling from nearby Clare Valley.
New Zealand — Marlborough
The defining region for Sauvignon Blanc: pungent, zesty, with passion fruit and grapefruit. Also producing excellent Pinot Noir and sparkling wines.
Wine Tasting Journal 6
Reference Tasting Vocabulary
Tasting Terminology
Speak the language of wine
Nose
The aroma of the wine — what you smell before you taste. Swirl the glass to release aromas, then take short sniffs. The nose reveals the grape variety, oak treatment, and age.
Palate
The flavors and textures you experience as the wine enters your mouth. Take a small sip and let it coat your tongue. Note sweetness, acidity, tannin, body, and flavor intensity.
Finish
The lingering flavors and sensations after you swallow. Can be short (fades quickly), medium, or long (lasts 30+ seconds). A great finish evolves and lingers.
Tannins
Natural compounds from grape skins, seeds, and oak that create a drying, astringent sensation in your mouth. Essential for red wine structure and aging. Softer in older wines.
Acidity
The crisp, tart quality that makes your mouth water. High in cool-climate whites and reds like Sangiovese. Acidity gives wine freshness and makes it food-friendly.
Body
The weight and texture of the wine in your mouth — light (like water), medium (like whole milk), or full (like cream). Determined by alcohol, tannin, sugar, and extract.
Terroir
The unique combination of soil, climate, and topography that gives a wine its sense of place. A wine that tastes like where it's from has terroir expression.
Vintage
The year the grapes were harvested. Weather varies year to year, so the same wine from different vintages can taste quite different. Great vintages age longer and cost more.
