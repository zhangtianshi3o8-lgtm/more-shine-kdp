How to Use This Journal
1
Record each stargazing session as soon as possible while the details are fresh. Fill in the fields on the left page, then note your observations on the right.
2
Use the sketch circle to draw what you see through your eyepiece — star patterns, brightness, and positions of objects.
3
Rate sky conditions by seeing and transparency. Over time you will learn which nights are worth setting up your gear.
4
Log details about your equipment on the next page so you can track which setups work best for different objects.
5
Use the summary pages at the back to tally your sessions, hours under the stars, and objects observed.
6
This journal fits 35 observation sessions. Bring it along on every night out under the sky.
Pro Tip
Use a red light flashlight to protect your night vision when filling out this journal. It takes about 30 minutes for your eyes to fully dark-adapt.

Constellation Guide
Twelve major constellations to find
1
Ursa Major
The Great Bear. Home to the Big Dipper asterism; the pointer stars point to Polaris. Visible year-round in the northern sky.
2
Ursa Minor
The Little Bear. Contains Polaris, the North Star, around which the northern sky appears to rotate. Circumpolar for most northern observers.
3
Cassiopeia
The Queen. Distinctive W or M shape. Circumpolar in the north. Rich in star clusters and lies in the Milky Way band.
4
Cepheus
The King. Resembles a house or pointed hat. Circumpolar for northern latitudes. Home to the Garnet Star and variable star Delta Cephei.
5
Draco
The Dragon. Long winding constellation winding between the Big and Little Dippers. Contains Thuban, a former pole star.
6
Orion
The Hunter. Most recognizable constellation, visible in winter. Belt of three stars; sword holds the famous Orion Nebula.
7
Canis Major
The Greater Dog. Contains Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky. Best seen in winter months below Orion.
8
Taurus
The Bull. Contains the Pleiades (Seven Sisters) star cluster and the Hyades. The red star Aldebaran marks the bull's eye.
9
Gemini
The Twins. Marked by bright stars Castor and Pollux. Site of a major meteor shower in December.
10
Leo
The Lion. Distinctive backwards-question-mark (the Sickle) marks the lion's mane. Bright star Regulus anchors the constellation.
11
Scorpius
The Scorpion. Striking summer constellation with the red star Antares. Curved tail resembles a scorpion's stinger.
12
Lyra
The Lyre. Small but bright, led by Vega. Contains the Ring Nebula, a classic planetary nebula for telescopes.

Give your eyes at least 20-30 minutes to dark-adapt. Avoid white light at all costs — use only red light to preserve your night vision throughout the session.
