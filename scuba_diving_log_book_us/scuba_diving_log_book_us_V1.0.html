How to Use This Log Book
Your personal record of every dive
Why Keep a Dive Log?
A dive log is more than a record — it is proof of your experience, a tool for tracking your air consumption and comfort at depth, and a passport to deeper adventures. Dive operators worldwide require log books as evidence of your certification level and dive count. Your log tells the story of your underwater journey.
What to Record Every Dive
1. Log immediately. Record depth, time, and air while the details are fresh. Waiting even a few hours means forgetting key data.
2. Track your gas. Start pressure, end pressure, and breathing rate reveal your air consumption trends and help you plan future dives.
3. Record conditions. Water temperature, visibility, current, and surge affect every dive. Note what gear configuration worked best.
4. Note the wildlife. The creatures you encounter make each dive unique. From reef fish to pelagics, your log becomes a marine life journal.
5. Reflect on skills. What went well? What needs improvement? Buoyancy, navigation, air management — track your growth as a diver.
Pro Tip: Use a waterproof dive slate underwater to note depth, time, and air. Transfer the data to your log book on the surface.
Scuba Diving Log Book 3
Reference Safety Essentials
Dive Safety Essentials
Plan the dive, dive the plan
Plan Your Dive
Agree on max depth, bottom time, and air turn-around point with your buddy before entering the water.
Buddy System
Stay close to your buddy. Perform buddy checks before every dive — BCD, Weights, Releases, Air, Final check.
Ascend Slowly
Never exceed 30 feet (9 meters) per minute. A safety stop at 15 feet for 3 minutes is mandatory on every dive deeper than 30 feet.
Monitor Your Air
Check your pressure gauge frequently. Turn the dive when you reach the agreed reserve — typically 500 psi / 50 bar.
Equalize Early
Equalize your ears before feeling discomfort. If you cannot equalize, ascend slightly and try again. Never force it.
Dive Within Your Limits
Stay within your certification level. If something feels wrong, call the dive. Any diver can end a dive at any time for any reason.
Remember: This log book is a personal record. It does not replace proper training. Always follow your certifying agency's standards and local diving regulations.
Scuba Diving Log Book 4
Reference Dive Types
Types of Diving
Know your dive environments
Reef Dive
The most popular recreational dive. Explore coral gardens teeming with marine life. Depths typically 15-80 feet.
Wall Dive
Follow a vertical drop-off into the deep blue. Spectacular visibility and pelagic encounters. Watch your depth gauge closely.
Wreck Dive
Explore sunken ships, planes, and artificial reefs. Requires additional training for penetration. Rich in history and marine life.
Drift Dive
Float with the current while the boat follows. Effortless and exhilarating. Requires a surface marker buoy (SMB).
Night Dive
Experience the underwater world after dark. Different marine life emerges. Requires a primary dive light and backup.
Cavern/Cave Dive
Explore overhead environments. Requires specialized cave diving certification. Always maintain a continuous guideline to the surface.
Deep Dive
Beyond 60 feet (18m). Requires deep diver specialty training. Shorter bottom times due to nitrogen absorption.
Shore Dive
Enter from the beach. Economical and flexible. Plan your exit point and tide conditions carefully.
Scuba Diving Log Book 5
Reference Hand Signals
Essential Hand Signals
Silent communication underwater
OK
Touch thumb and index finger to form a circle. Used both on surface and underwater.
Stop / Hold
Palm facing outward, fingers up. Signal to stop and hold position.
Go Up / Ascend
Thumb up. Signal that you or the group should ascend.
Go Down / Descend
Thumb down. Signal to descend or go deeper.
Out of Air
Hand slashing across the throat. Emergency signal — buddy must share air immediately.
Low on Air
Clenched fist moved in a cutting motion across the chest. Signal that air supply is running low.
How Much Air?
Fingers and thumb tapping together. Asking buddy to show their pressure gauge.
Something Wrong
Flat hand waved side to side, palm down. Followed by pointing to the problem area.
Look at Me
Point two fingers at own eyes. Direct buddy's attention to you.
Danger / Hazard
Closed fist. Used to warn of danger or signal buddy to pay attention.
Signal Tip: Always respond to a signal — even a simple "OK" back confirms you saw and understood. Underwater communication is a two-way agreement.
Scuba Diving Log Book 6
Reference Gas Management
Understanding Air Consumption
Track your breathing rate to dive smarter
Why Track Your Air?
Your breathing rate (Surface Air Consumption, or SAC rate) is one of the most important personal metrics in diving. Knowing your SAC helps you plan dive times, choose the right cylinder, and dive with confidence. It varies with depth, exertion, stress, and experience.
How to Calculate SAC Rate
SAC (psi/min) = Air Used (psi) ÷ Bottom Time (min) ÷ Pressure at Depth (ATA)
Example: You start with 3000 psi, finish with 1000 psi after a 40-minute dive at an average depth of 33 feet (2 ATA).
Air used = 3000 − 1000 = 2000 psi
SAC = 2000 ÷ 40 ÷ 2 = 25 psi/min
Typical SAC Rates
Experience LevelSAC Rate (psi/min)SAC Rate (L/min)
Beginner25-3520-28
Intermediate20-2816-22
Experienced15-2212-18
Expert / Technical12-1810-15
Track Your Progress: Record start pressure, end pressure, depth, and bottom time for every dive. Over time, you will see your SAC rate improve as your buoyancy and relaxation underwater get better.
