Getting Started More Shine Press
How to Use This Logbook
Every find has a story worth preserving
Why Document Your Finds?
Metal detecting is as much about the story as the object. Without records, details fade — where you found it, what settings you used, how deep it was. A logbook helps you identify patterns, return to productive sites, and preserve the provenance that gives each find meaning.
Tips for Better Records
1. Record GPS coordinates. Mark every productive spot. Phone GPS apps work well. Future you will thank present you when you want to revisit a site that produced silver.
2. Note detector settings. Record sensitivity, discrimination, frequency, and search mode. Different settings find different things. Knowing what worked helps you repeat success.
3. Log depth and signal. Record the depth reading and the signal tone (high/low/choppy/whisper). This builds your ear for interpreting signals before you dig.
4. Photograph in situ. Take a photo of the find in the hole before removing it. Context and soil conditions help with identification and dating.
5. Clean carefully. Wrong cleaning destroys value. Note what method you used and the results. Coins especially need gentle handling.
6. Research your finds. Spend time identifying items. Old coins, buckles, buttons, and relics all have stories. Note your research findings in the notes section.
Metal Detecting Logbook 3
Reference Metal Identification
Identifying Common Finds
What the ground gives up
Metal Type Signal Tone Identification Tips
Copper Med-High Smooth Green patina (verdigris). Pennies, old tokens, copper artifacts. Conductive, strong signal.
Silver High Bright Tarnishes black/brown. Coins, jewelry. Very conductive. High-pitched bell tone. Rings clear.
Gold Low-Med Smooth Does not tarnish. Rings, coins, nuggets. Low conductive. Often same VDI as foil/pulltab.
Brass Med-High Smooth Yellow-gold color. Buckles, buttons, cartridge cases. Strong, smooth signal. Similar to copper.
Bronze Med Smooth Brownish-green patina. Ancient coins, statues, relics. Harder than copper, similar signal.
Iron Low Choppy Rusts red-brown. Nails, wire, tools, horseshoes. Usually discriminated out. Jumpy signal.
Steel Low Choppy Silver-gray, rusts. Modern items, bottle caps, screws. Magnetic. Loud, jumpy, often double blips.
Lead Low Soft Heavy, dull gray-white. Bullets, fishing weights, toy soldiers, pipes. Soft signal, heavy for size.
Aluminum Med-High Var. Lightweight, silver. Pull tabs, cans, foil. Frustrating — reads similar to gold. Light for size.
Nickel Low-Med Smooth Coins, buttons, buckles. Falls between foil and pull tab on VDI scale. Moderate conductivity.
Zinc Med Var. Bluish-white. Modern pennies (1982+). Corrodes with white crust in soil. Crumbly if buried long.
Platinum Low Smooth Silver-white. Rare — rings, jewelry. Very low conductivity. Similar to iron but smooth tone.
Signal = strength relative to other metals. Tone = audio character. VDI (Visual Discrimination Indicator) numbers vary by detector brand. Always dig questionable targets — the best finds often mask as trash.
Metal Detecting Logbook 4
Reference Cleaning & Preservation
Cleaning & Preservation
Handle with care — you only get one chance
The Golden Rule of Cleaning
When in doubt, do nothing. A dirty, uncleaned coin with original patina is worth more than a cleaned, scratched one. Aggressive cleaning destroys collector value instantly. Research before you touch anything that might be valuable.
Material Safe? Cleaning Method
Coins (Valuable) NO Do not clean. Leave patina intact. Professional conservation only. Handling reduces grade.
Coins (Common) Soak Soak in distilled water 24-48 hrs. Gently brush with soft toothbrush. Never scrub. Olive oil soak for stubborn dirt (days to weeks).
Silver Gentle Soak in water. Baking soda paste, gentle rub. Aluminum foil + baking soda bath removes tarnish. Never use abrasives on numismatic pieces.
Copper Careful Soak in olive oil for weeks. Soft brush only. Never remove green patina — it protects the surface and adds character.
Brass/Bronze Careful Soak in soapy water. Brass brushes only if heavily corroded. Preserve patina on relics. Wax to protect after cleaning.
Iron Yes Wire brush to remove loose rust. Soak in evaporust or molasses/water (weeks). Dry immediately. Coat with wax or oil to prevent re-rusting.
Lead Gentle Soak in water. Very soft — never scrub hard. White corrosion can be gently picked off. Handle carefully — lead is soft and toxic.
Gold Yes Rinse in water. Gentle dish soap. Gold does not tarnish. Ultrasonic cleaner is safe. Jewelry polish cloth for shine.
Relics (Unknown) Research Identify material first. When in doubt, leave dirt on. Photograph before cleaning. Consult experts for potentially historic items.
What Never to Do
Don'tWhy
Polish coinsRemoves toning and destroys numismatic value completely.
Use acids/chemicalsEats metal surface. Vinegar, lemon juice, commercial cleaners all strip patina.
Scrub with abrasivesCreates hairline scratches that are visible under magnification. Lowers grade.
Tumble cleanDestroys surface detail on coins and delicate relics. Only for modern clad coins.
Use steel brushesScratches soft metals (copper, silver, lead). Embeds steel particles.
Metal Detecting Logbook 5
Reference Permissions & Laws
Permissions & Laws
Always hunt legally and ethically
The Most Important Rule
Always get permission before detecting on private property. Trespassing is a crime. On public land, know the regulations before you go. When in doubt, ask the landowner or managing authority. A good reputation benefits every detectorist.
Private Property
1. Always get written permission from the landowner. A handshake is good, but a signed permission slip protects you.
2. Agree in advance on what happens to finds. Some landowners want a share. Be fair and build relationships.
3. Fill all holes. Leave the property as you found it. Carry a plug-cutting tool and learn proper digging technique.
4. Offer to share interesting finds or photos with the landowner. Good relationships lead to return visits and referrals.
Public Lands (United States)
5. National Parks, Monuments, and Historic Sites: Detecting is generally prohibited. Do not detect in these areas.
6. State Parks: Regulations vary by state. Many require permits. Check with the state parks department before detecting.
7. City and County Parks: Often allowed, but check local ordinances. Some cities prohibit detecting or require permits.
8. Schoolyards and sports fields: Usually public property but may have restrictions. Check with the school district or recreation department.
9. Beaches: Many public beaches allow detecting. Some states require permits. Research the specific beach rules — rules differ even within the same county.
Ethical Considerations
10. Report significant historical finds to local historians or museums. Context matters for understanding history.
11. Remove trash you dig. Every pull tab you take out makes the site cleaner for the next visit.
12. Respect archaeological sites. If you find something potentially significant, photograph in place, note location, and contact professionals.
13. Follow the Detectorist Code: leave no trace, fill holes, respect property, share knowledge, and represent the hobby well.
Metal Detecting Logbook 6
Reference Detector Settings Guide
Detector Settings Guide
Understanding your machine
Setting Range What It Does
Sensitivity 1-10 / Max Detection depth and signal strength. Higher = deeper but more noise/interference. Lower in mineralized soil or near power lines.
Discrimination 0-100 Filters out unwanted metals (iron, foil). Higher = rejects more but may miss good targets. Low discrimination = dig everything.
Ground Balance 0-100 Tunes out mineralization in soil. Manual or auto. Essential in mineralized ground. Wrong GB = reduced depth and false signals.
Threshold Off-Max Background hum. Set barely audible. Changes in hum indicate targets. Key for small/deep targets in quiet hunting.
Tone ID 1-4 / Multi Number of audio tones for different metals. More tones = more info but harder to learn. Multi-tone gives pitch per target ID number.
Frequency 3-100 kHz Low (5 kHz) = depth, silver, high conductors. High (18+ kHz) = small gold, low conductors, separation in trash. Multi-frequency does both.
Notch Filter On/Off Rejects specific target ID categories while accepting others. Useful to notch out pull tabs while keeping nickel/gold range.
Pinpoint Mode Toggle Non-motion mode that locates exact target position. Signal gets louder as coil centers over target. Use before digging.
Search Mode Presets Pre-configured settings (Coins, Jewelry, Relic, Beach, All Metal). Good starting point. Customize as you learn your machine.
VDI Number Guide (approximate — varies by detector)
VDI RangeLikely Target
-10 to 0Iron (nails, wire, horseshoes)
0 to 15Foil, tiny gold nuggets, small bits
15 to 25Pull tabs, nickels, small gold rings
25 to 40Aluminum, screw caps, large gold
40 to 60Pennies (copper), small brass, shotgun shells
60 to 80Dimes, quarters, halves, silver coins
80 to 99Large silver, copper, brass buttons, silver dollars
Pro Tip: The best detectorists dig everything in new sites. Once you understand the trash pattern, start using discrimination. Silver and gold can hide in the same VDI range as trash — trust your ears and dig the smooth, repeatable signals.
